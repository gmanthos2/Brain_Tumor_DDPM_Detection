% 1. INTRODUCTION

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

Medical imaging is central to diagnosing neurological disorders, with MRI serving as the primary modality for detecting structural brain abnormalities~\cite{baur2021autoencoders}. However, automated analysis faces persistent challenges: annotated datasets are scarce, class distributions are heavily imbalanced, and privacy regulations constrain data sharing~\cite{yi2019generative}. These limitations motivate \textit{unsupervised} anomaly detection, which learns normality exclusively from healthy data and identifies pathology as deviations from this distribution~\cite{baur2021autoencoders, schlegl2019f}.

Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Models (DDPMs)~\cite{ho2020denoising} have demonstrated superior sample quality and training stability over GANs~\cite{goodfellow2014generative, dhariwal2021diffusion}. Latent Diffusion Models (LDMs)~\cite{rombach2022high} further reduce computational costs by operating in a compressed latent space. For medical anomaly detection, diffusion-based methods reconstruct pathological inputs as healthy counterparts, using the reconstruction error as an anomaly signal~\cite{wyatt2022anoddpm, wolleb2022diffusion, sanchez2022healthy}---requiring \textit{no anomaly labels during training}.

In this work, we present a complete latent diffusion pipeline for brain MRI analysis serving a dual purpose: (1)~anomaly detection via reconstruction-error-based tumor localization, and (2)~synthetic brain MRI generation for data augmentation. Our contributions are:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item A two-stage latent diffusion architecture combining a MONAI-based VAE with a class-conditional U-Net DDPM, using CFG and DDIM sampling.
  \item A systematic ablation study across 120 configurations---10~checkpoints, 4~noise levels, and 3~guidance scales---characterizing detection sensitivity to each factor.
  \item Quantitative evaluation of both anomaly detection (image- and pixel-level AUROC) and synthetic image quality (FID, intra-set diversity).
  \item Explainability via self-attention maps and denoising trajectories, plus a discussion of ethical considerations.
\end{enumerate}

% 2. RELATED WORK

\section{Related Work}
\label{sec:related_work}

\subsection{Denoising Diffusion Models}

DDPMs~\cite{ho2020denoising} define a forward Markov chain that corrupts data with Gaussian noise over $T$ timesteps, and a learned reverse process that denoises samples. The training objective is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L} = \mathbb{E}_{t, \mathbf{x}_0, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}} \left[ \| \boldsymbol{\epsilon} - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t) \|^2 \right],
\label{eq:ddpm_loss}
\end{equation}
where $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta$ is a U-Net trained to predict the noise added at timestep $t$. DDIM~\cite{song2021denoising} enables deterministic sampling with fewer steps (e.g., 50 instead of 1000). LDMs~\cite{rombach2022high} apply diffusion in a compressed latent space via a pretrained autoencoder. Classifier-Free Guidance (CFG)~\cite{ho2022classifier} enables conditional generation without a separate classifier:
\begin{equation}
\tilde{\boldsymbol{\epsilon}} = \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \varnothing) + w \cdot \left( \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, c) - \boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{x}_t, t, \varnothing) \right),
\label{eq:cfg}
\end{equation}
where $w$ is the guidance scale and $\varnothing$ denotes the null class token.

\subsection{Anomaly Detection in Medical Imaging}

Unsupervised anomaly detection follows a reconstruction-based paradigm: a model trained on healthy data identifies anomalies as regions with high reconstruction error~\cite{baur2021autoencoders}. Early VAE-based approaches~\cite{kingma2014auto} produced blurry reconstructions, while GAN methods like f-AnoGAN~\cite{schlegl2019f} suffered from instabilities. Diffusion-based alternatives have shown strong results: AnoDDPM~\cite{wyatt2022anoddpm} uses simplex noise and partial noising; Wolleb et al.~\cite{wolleb2022diffusion} condition the reverse process on a healthy class; Sanchez et al.~\cite{sanchez2022healthy} frame detection as counterfactual healthy generation; and Behrendt et al.~\cite{behrendt2024patched} introduced patched diffusion models for improved localization.

\subsection{Synthetic Medical Image Generation}

GANs have been applied for medical synthesis~\cite{yi2019generative}, but instability and mode collapse remain problematic. Diffusion models show particular promise: Pinaya et al.~\cite{pinaya2022brain} demonstrated brain image generation using latent diffusion with MONAI~\cite{pinaya2023generative}, and Dorjsembe et al.~\cite{dorjsembe2022three} extended DDPMs to 3D medical volumes.

\section{Methodology}
\label{sec:methodology}

Our pipeline follows a two-stage latent diffusion architecture. Stage~1 employs a VAE to compress brain MRI images into a compact latent space. Stage~2 trains a class-conditional DDPM in this latent space. At inference, the system supports anomaly detection via partial noising and healthy-conditioned reconstruction, and synthetic image generation via full DDIM sampling.

\subsection{Variational Autoencoder (Stage 1)}
\label{sec:vae}

The VAE uses the \texttt{AutoencoderKL} architecture from MONAI~\cite{monai2022, pinaya2023generative}, mapping images from $\mathbb{R}^{1 \times 256 \times 256}$ to $\mathbb{R}^{4 \times 32 \times 32}$ ($8\times$ spatial downsampling). The encoder uses channel progression $[64, 128, 256, 256]$ with 2~residual blocks per level and self-attention at the two lowest resolutions; the decoder mirrors this symmetrically. The training objective is:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{L}_{\text{VAE}} = \mathcal{L}_{\text{L1}} + 0.5 \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{LPIPS}} + 10^{-6} \cdot \mathcal{L}_{\text{KL}},
\label{eq:vae_loss}
\end{equation}
where the low KL weight prioritizes reconstruction fidelity. Training ran for 150 epochs with effective batch size 32, using AdamW ($\text{lr} = 10^{-4}$) and mixed-precision training.

\subsection{Latent Space Normalization}
\label{sec:normalization}

Raw VAE latents exhibited per-channel mean $\approx 0.86$ and standard deviation $\approx 7.2$---far from $\mathcal{N}(0, 1)$ assumed by the DDPM noise schedule. Without correction, the signal-to-noise ratio at each timestep is miscalibrated, producing pure noise during generation. We apply z-score normalization using precomputed statistics:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{z}_{\text{norm}} = \frac{\mathbf{z}_{\text{raw}} - \boldsymbol{\mu}}{\boldsymbol{\sigma}}, \qquad
\mathbf{z}_{\text{raw}} = \mathbf{z}_{\text{norm}} \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + \boldsymbol{\mu}.
\label{eq:normalization}
\end{equation}
Normalization is applied before DDPM training; denormalization after DDIM sampling and before VAE decoding.

\subsection{Denoising Diffusion Probabilistic Model (Stage 2)}
\label{sec:ddpm}

The DDPM operates in the normalized latent space using a U-Net~\cite{ronneberger2015u} for noise prediction on $32 \times 32 \times 4$ tensors.

\paragraph{Architecture and Conditioning.} The U-Net uses base channel width 128 with multipliers $[1, 2, 4, 4]$, yielding feature maps of $[128, 256, 512, 512]$ across four resolution levels ($32 \rightarrow 16 \rightarrow 8 \rightarrow 4$). Each level has 2~residual blocks with GroupNorm and SiLU. Multi-head self-attention~\cite{vaswani2017attention} (4~heads) is applied at resolutions $16$ and $8$. A middle block with residual--attention--residual structure processes the $4 \times 4$ bottleneck. Timestep conditioning uses sinusoidal embeddings projected through a two-layer MLP. Class conditioning employs a learned embedding table with 3~entries: healthy (0), anomalous (1), and null ($\varnothing$). Time and class embeddings are summed and injected via additive conditioning. Because the model is trained exclusively on healthy images (Section~\ref{sec:dataset}), only the healthy and null embeddings receive gradient updates; the anomalous embedding remains at its random initialization and is never used at inference.

\paragraph{Schedule and Training.} A linear noise schedule with $\beta_{\text{start}} = 10^{-4}$, $\beta_{\text{end}} = 0.02$ over $T = 1000$ timesteps is used. The model is trained for 200K steps (batch size 32) with AdamW (lr $2 \times 10^{-4}$, weight decay 0.01), gradient clipping at norm 1.0, and mixed precision. An EMA~\cite{polyak1992acceleration} of weights (decay 0.9999) is used for all inference. Training loss converged to $\approx$0.025--0.035. Gradient checkpointing is enabled to fit within 8~GB VRAM.

\subsection{Classifier-Free Guidance}
\label{sec:cfg}

During training, class labels are randomly replaced with $\varnothing$ with probability $p = 0.1$, so the model learns both a conditional prediction $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, c{=}\text{healthy})$ and an unconditional prediction $\boldsymbol{\epsilon}_\theta(\mathbf{z}_t, t, \varnothing)$. Because training uses only healthy labels, the conditional and unconditional branches differ solely through the presence or absence of the healthy class embedding (dropped with $p = 0.1$). At inference, the guided prediction follows Equation~\ref{eq:cfg}, where $w$ controls the degree to which the healthy class embedding steers the reconstruction away from the unconditional estimate. In practice, because the unconditional branch has seen 90\% healthy-conditioned and 10\% null-conditioned examples---all from the same healthy data distribution---the gap between conditional and unconditional noise estimates is small, which limits the practical effect of $w$ on discrimination (see Section~\ref{sec:ablation}).

\subsection{Anomaly Detection Pipeline}
\label{sec:anomaly_pipeline}

The anomaly detection process is formalized in Algorithm~\ref{alg:anomaly}. Given an input MRI $\mathbf{x}$, the pipeline encodes it via the VAE, normalizes, adds noise at timestep $t_{\text{start}}$, reconstructs a healthy version via DDIM with CFG, denormalizes and decodes, then computes the anomaly map as $|\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}|$ smoothed with a Gaussian filter ($\sigma = 2.0$). The global anomaly score is the mean raw L1 error.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Diffusion-Based Anomaly Detection}
\label{alg:anomaly}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE Input image $\mathbf{x}$, noise level $t_{\text{start}}$, guidance scale $w$, DDIM steps $S$
\ENSURE Anomaly map $\mathbf{A}$, anomaly score $s$
\STATE $\mathbf{z}_0 \leftarrow \text{VAE}_{\text{enc}}(\mathbf{x})$ \COMMENT{Encode to latent space}
\STATE $\mathbf{z}_0^{\text{norm}} \leftarrow (\mathbf{z}_0 - \boldsymbol{\mu}) / \boldsymbol{\sigma}$ \COMMENT{Normalize}
\STATE $\boldsymbol{\epsilon} \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$
\STATE $\mathbf{z}_{t} \leftarrow \sqrt{\bar{\alpha}_{t}} \, \mathbf{z}_0^{\text{norm}} + \sqrt{1 - \bar{\alpha}_{t}} \, \boldsymbol{\epsilon}$ \COMMENT{Forward noise}
\STATE $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0^{\text{norm}} \leftarrow \text{DDIM}(\mathbf{z}_{t},\, c{=}\text{healthy},\, w,\, S)$ \COMMENT{Healthy reconstruction}
\STATE $\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0 \leftarrow \hat{\mathbf{z}}_0^{\text{norm}} \cdot \boldsymbol{\sigma} + \boldsymbol{\mu}$ \COMMENT{Denormalize}
\STATE $\hat{\mathbf{x}} \leftarrow \text{VAE}_{\text{dec}}(\hat{\mathbf{z}}_0)$ \COMMENT{Decode to pixel space}
\STATE $\mathbf{A}_{\text{raw}} \leftarrow |\mathbf{x} - \hat{\mathbf{x}}|$
\STATE $\mathbf{A} \leftarrow \text{GaussianSmooth}(\mathbf{A}_{\text{raw}}, \sigma{=}2.0)$
\STATE $s \leftarrow \text{mean}(\mathbf{A}_{\text{raw}})$ \COMMENT{Global anomaly score}
\RETURN $\mathbf{A}, s$
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The parameter $t_{\text{start}}$ controls the trade-off between anomaly destruction and anatomy preservation: higher values destroy more of the original signal (including healthy structure), while lower values may fail to remove the anomaly. This trade-off is analyzed in Section~\ref{sec:results}.

\subsection{Synthetic Image Generation}
\label{sec:generation}

For generation, full DDIM sampling starts from pure Gaussian noise $\mathbf{z}_T \sim \mathcal{N}(\mathbf{0}, \mathbf{I})$, conditioned on the healthy class with 50 deterministic steps ($\eta = 0$). The result is denormalized and decoded to produce a $256 \times 256$ grayscale brain MRI.

\section{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:experiments}

\subsection{Dataset}
\label{sec:dataset}

We use the Brain Tumor Detection dataset~\cite{bhuvaji2020brain} from Kaggle, comprising $\sim$1,500 healthy and $\sim$1,500 tumor-bearing 2D brain MRI slices. Ground truth tumor annotations are provided as VIA polygon masks in JSON format. All images are preprocessed to $256 \times 256$ grayscale and normalized to $[-1, 1]$. Because the DDPM is trained \textit{exclusively} on healthy images following the unsupervised paradigm, the dataset split of 70\% training, 15\% validation, and 15\% testing is applied solely to the healthy images. To form the final test set for evaluation, the 225 healthy test images are combined with 450 of the unused anomalous images.

\subsection{Implementation Details}
\label{sec:implementation}

The pipeline is implemented in PyTorch 2.12 with MONAI~\cite{monai2022} for the VAE. All experiments run on a single NVIDIA RTX 4060 (8~GB VRAM), using gradient checkpointing, automatic mixed precision, and consolidated latent storage ($\sim$17~MB). \subsection{Evaluation Metrics}
\label{sec:metrics}

\paragraph{Anomaly Detection.} At the image level, each image receives a scalar anomaly score (mean raw L1 error); we report AUROC, AUPRC, F1, sensitivity, and specificity. At the pixel level, we compute AUROC using ground truth polygon masks.

\paragraph{Synthetic Image Quality.} We assess generation quality using FID~\cite{heusel2017gans} and intra-set diversity (pairwise LPIPS~\cite{zhang2018perceptual} between generated images). For FID, grayscale images are channel-replicated to three-channel pseudo-RGB before being passed through an ImageNet-pretrained Inception-v3 network; features are extracted from the final pooling layer and the Fr\'echet distance is computed between real and generated feature distributions. We note that ImageNet-calibrated features are not ideal for single-channel medical imagery, so the absolute FID value should be interpreted with caution.

\paragraph{Effect of Guidance Scale ($w$).} AUROC remains stable across guidance values (varying $<$0.002). \section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

\paragraph{Anomaly Detection Mechanism.} Our results confirm that latent diffusion models can detect brain tumors through reconstruction-based scoring without anomaly labels. The DDPM, trained only on healthy anatomy, ``corrects'' pathological regions during healthy-conditioned reconstruction, producing measurable error in tumor areas.

\paragraph{Critical Role of $t_{\text{start}}$.} The ablation study identifies $t_{\text{start}}$ as the dominant factor in detection performance. Sufficient noise must destroy the anomaly signal, but excessive noise also destroys healthy features, causing hallucinated anatomy and false positives. Our optimal $t_{\text{start}} = 150$ ($T = 1000$) balances these objectives, consistent with findings in AnoDDPM~\cite{wyatt2022anoddpm}.

\paragraph{Guidance Scale as a Clinical Tuning Knob.} As noted in Section~\ref{sec:cfg}, single-class training means CFG interpolates between two nearly identical noise estimates---conditional on the healthy embedding versus the unconditional null---so AUROC is largely unaffected by $w$. The guidance scale does, however, modulate the sensitivity--specificity operating point: at $w = 3.0$, the model achieves 88\% sensitivity at the cost of more false positives; at $w = 7.5$, the balance shifts toward specificity. This tunability is valuable for clinical deployment where cost asymmetries between missed tumors and false alarms vary by application.

\paragraph{Generation Quality and Comparison.} The FID of 144.43 should be interpreted with the caveats discussed in Section~\ref{sec:metrics}: the Inception network was trained on ImageNet and receives channel-replicated grayscale inputs, so the absolute value is not directly comparable to FID scores reported on natural-image benchmarks. Our image-level AUROC of 0.756 falls within ranges reported by comparable methods: AnoDDPM~\cite{wyatt2022anoddpm} achieves 0.70--0.83 and Wolleb et al.~\cite{wolleb2022diffusion} report 0.74--0.86. Our pixel-level AUROC of 0.894 is competitive despite operating at coarse latent resolution ($32 \times 32$) upsampled to $256 \times 256$.

\paragraph{Inference Efficiency.} With CFG and $S = 50$ DDIM steps, each pass requires $2S = 100$ U-Net evaluations. Operating in the $32 \times 32$ latent space reduces FLOPs by $\sim$$50\times$ versus pixel space, and DDIM's $20\times$ step reduction yields $\sim$$1000\times$ overall speedup. Inference completes in $\sim$300--350~ms per image on an RTX 4060, with $\sim$52M total parameters (35M U-Net + 17M VAE).

\paragraph{Limitations.} (1)~\textbf{2D processing} ignores volumetric context. (2)~The \textbf{limited dataset} ($\sim$3,000 images from a single source) constrains anatomical diversity. (3)~\textbf{No demographic metadata} precludes fairness analysis. (4)~The $8\times$ VAE compression creates a \textbf{resolution bottleneck}, limiting localization to $\sim$$8 \times 8$ pixel patches. (5)~\textbf{No clinical validation}---deployment requires prospective validation with radiologist assessment.

\paragraph{Ethical and Regulatory Considerations.} Diffusion models trained on small datasets risk memorizing individual examples~\cite{akbar2023beware, dar2024investigating}; mitigations include perceptual similarity monitoring and differential privacy. The absence of demographic metadata precludes fairness analysis. Synthetic images must carry provenance labels and not substitute for real diagnostic data. Clinical application requires compliance with the EU AI Act and FDA SaMD guidance. This work is a research prototype and is \textbf{not suitable for clinical diagnosis}.

% ============================================================
% 7. CONCLUSION
% ============================================================
\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a two-stage latent diffusion pipeline combining a MONAI-based VAE with a class-conditional U-Net DDPM for brain MRI anomaly detection and synthetic image generation. The system achieves an image-level AUROC of 0.756 and pixel-level AUROC of 0.894, competitive with existing diffusion-based methods. Our ablation across 120 configurations reveals that $t_{\text{start}}$ dominates detection performance, while the guidance scale provides a clinically useful sensitivity--specificity trade-off. The model generates diverse synthetic brain MRIs (FID = 144.43). We further contribute explainability mechanisms and discuss ethical considerations. Future work includes 3D volumetric processing, larger multi-institutional datasets, multi-modal conditioning, and prospective clinical validation.
